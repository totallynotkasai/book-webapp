Chapter 5 The Lamp That Remembers

“A flame carried, not made,” said Mister Cobb. He was sitting in his chair by the fire with his feet in a bowl of hot water, and the Guildmaster of the Lumen Guild was sitting on a tin of brass polish because there was nowhere else, and neither of them seemed to find this strange. “Not a match, then. A match is made. Not Kindle either, I’d guess. Kindle is something you do. It has to be a flame that came from somewhere else and was brought.”

“From the end of the Long Road,” said Tam. “But nobody knows where that is.”

Bram had been quiet for a while, which was unusual. Now he cleared his throat.

“Our oven,” he said. “At the bakery. My great-grandad lit it the day the shop opened, eighty-one years ago, and it’s never once gone cold. Not even in the flood year. Mum banks it every night and rakes it every morning. It’s a family thing. We’re a bit silly about it.” He went red. “Only, I asked Gran once where Great-grandad got the fire from, and she said he walked up Wickery Lane with a taper and lit it from the lamp outside, because it was lucky.”

Everyone looked at the ledger.

Tam found the page. It took a long time, because eighty-one years was a lot of nights, but at last there it was, in a neat, square hand she had never seen before: Lamp 1, lit by Harold Ashby. Also lit from it this night, by leave, the oven of Albert Ollery, baker.

“Lamp one was lit from the First Lamp,” said Tam slowly. “And the oven was lit from lamp one. So the fire in your oven is the same fire. It’s been carried all the way.”

The Guildmaster stood up. She took a heavy iron key from the pocket of her grey coat and held it out to Tam.

I will be at the guildhall with every worker I have, putting oil lamps back on their posts and lighting them with matches until you come back.” She paused. “If you need someone to write their names in your book, they will be there.”

They took the ember from the Ollery oven an hour before midnight, in Mister Cobb’s little brass lantern with the dented lid. Bram’s mother raked it out herself with the long iron rake, a single coal that glowed orange-red, and laid it on a bed of fresh wick in the lantern’s cup, and closed the door on it, and kissed both of them on the top of the head as though they were going to school.

Wick was waiting on the step outside. When they set off, he came too. When Tam looked at her window, there was a third name in the party list.

The old town lay inside the old wall, and the old wall had not had lamps in twenty years, not since the Guild had taken over that Round and then quietly given up on it. The Murk inside was not a tide or a blanket. It was simply everything. Streets went into it and did not come out. The air was cold and smelled of nothing at all.

But the ember worked. Inside the lantern it glowed a steady orange, and around them, in a circle about as wide as a kitchen table, the grey drew back. The cobbles under their feet stayed the colour of cobbles. When Tam said “St Ember,” she could still remember what it meant.

Smudges came at them twice on the way, and Bram dealt with both lots, rising them like bread rolls one after another and laughing every time they popped. Wick dealt with a third, alone, by sitting in front of it and staring until it gave up. Tam lit nothing. There were no lamps left to light. She held the lantern very carefully and watched the ember and tried not to think about how small it was.

St Ember tower rose out of the grey quite suddenly, a black wall where there had been nothing. The clock face high above them had stopped at six minutes past eleven. The door at the bottom was small and arched and banded with iron, and the Guildmaster’s key turned in the lock as though it had been oiled yesterday.

Behind the door a stair went down in a spiral. They went down it.

At the bottom, where the stair opened into a round stone room, something was waiting for them.

It was tall, taller than a door, and made of the Murk the way a snowman is made of snow: grey and soft-edged and only roughly the shape of a person. It had no face, but Tam knew that it was looking at them. When it spoke, its voice sounded like a page being turned in a library a very long way away.

Where are you going?

Tam did not try to answer out of her head. Her head was full of grey. Instead she opened the ledger, as she had on the bridge, past three hundred and twelve years of names, to the very back, to the hidden page, and she read the answer out loud, exactly as it was written there.

“We are going to Lamp Zero. The First Lamp. Beneath the tower of St Ember. Lit by Agnes Wray with a flame carried, not made.” She took a breath. “And we are carrying the flame. It came from the oven of Albert Ollery, who lit it from lamp one, which was lit by Harold Ashby, which was lit from this lamp. It’s written down. All of it.”

The Warden was silent for a long moment. Then, slowly, the way fog lifts off a field on a winter morning, it stepped aside.

Behind it, in the centre of the room, on a plain stone pillar, stood a lamp.

It was not grand. Tam had expected something grand: gold, perhaps, or crystal, with carvings. It was an ordinary iron lamp with ordinary glass, a little older than the ones on the Wickery Round and a great deal dustier. Its wick was dry and black. There was no oil in its reservoir at all. Scratched into the iron of its base, in letters so worn they were almost smooth, were five words.

I burn for those written.

Tam held up the lantern. Inside it, the ember of the Ollery oven, carried eighty-one years and half a mile through the dark, flickered once and went the colour of a dying coal.
