Chapter 3 Murk on Saltgate Bridge

The Murk had grown while Tam was drinking Mister Cobb’s terrible tea. It had climbed the Saltgate steps and spilled onto the canal path, and lamp forty-seven, which she had lit herself not two hours ago, stood with its feet in grey. It was still burning, but thinly, the flame bent sideways as though something were leaning on it.

“That one’s yours?” Bram asked.

“Forty-seven. I lit it with a match.” Tam opened the ledger on her forearm, the way she had seen Mister Cobb carry it in the old days, and found the page with her name at the bottom. Below it, the paper was empty and waiting. She had no pen. She had a pencil stub she had used for tallying matches, and she licked the point the way her mother did and wrote: Lamp 47, lit by Tamsin Reed.

[System] Inscribe used. Lamp 47 has been written into the Ledger. Light 4 / 5.

The flame straightened. It did not grow, exactly; it seemed to become more itself, a clear, definite gold, and the grey around the lamp post drew back from it in a neat circle as if someone had drawn a line on the ground with chalk.

Bram whistled.

“Every lamp,” said Tam, mostly to herself. “Don’t skip one.”

They went down the steps together. Wick went first, which neither of them had asked him to do. At the bottom the Murk was ankle-deep, then knee-deep, cool and faintly damp, like walking through a cellar. The sounds of the town fell away behind them. Tam found that she had to keep reminding herself what she was doing. Pim. Bridge. Lamps. Each time she said it in her head, the words seemed a little further away, as if they were written on a page that someone kept turning.

[System] You are inside the Murk. Wits check passed (9). You remember why you are here.

“Bram,” she said. “Why are we here?”

He frowned. “Bread,” he said uncertainly. Then his face cleared. “No. Pim. My brother. Tam, that was horrible, I’d just forgotten.”

“Hold onto my sleeve,” said Tam. “And keep saying his name.”

Lamp forty-eight stood at the foot of the steps, the one that had refused to light. This time Tam did not try Kindle straight away. She struck a match first, an ordinary match from an ordinary box, cupped it in her hands until it was burning well, and then lit the wick with it the old-fashioned way. The flame was small and nervous. Before it could falter she wrote it down. Lamp 48, lit by Tamsin Reed.

The Murk pulled back in a circle three yards wide.

[System] Lamp 48 lit and inscribed. Light 3 / 5. You gained 20 XP.

That was how they crossed the bridge: one lamp at a time. Match, flame, pencil. Bram held the lantern and the matchbox and said “Pim, Pim, Pim” under his breath like a song. Wick walked ahead of them along the parapet, a grey shape in the grey, stopping at each lamp post to sit and wait, as if he knew the order of the Round as well as Mister Cobb did.

The smudges came at the third lamp.

There were five of them, rolling out of the grey along the cobbles, and they were bigger than the one on the steps, the size of footstools now. Where they passed, the colour went out of everything. The red paint on the bridge railings turned to the colour of ash. A dropped ribbon on the cobbles went from blue to nothing at all.

“Weak to warmth,” said Bram, who was reading his own copy of the window. “I’ve got warmth. I’ve got six of it.”

“What does it do?”

“Usually it makes the oven hotter.” He set down the lantern, rubbed his floury hands together, and held them out towards the nearest smudge as if he were warming them at a fire. “Proof!”

The air in front of his hands shimmered. The smudge stopped rolling, puffed up like a loaf left too long by the stove, went from grey to pale grey to almost white, and burst with a soft sound, like somebody blowing out a candle in another room.

[System] Bram Ollery used Proof on a Smudge. The Smudge has risen and dispersed. Party gained 25 XP.

“It makes things rise,” said Bram, astonished. “Tam, it makes things rise!”

“Do it again!”

He did it twice more, and then his hands began to shake and his window turned faintly red at the edges. Tam had already struck a match and was waving it at the fourth smudge like a sword; it was not a very good sword, but the smudge did not like it. Wick, meanwhile, had made himself very large and very loud, and the fifth smudge, the big one, was hanging back in the grey at the far end of the bridge, waiting.

Behind it, in the middle of the road, sat a small boy on an upturned basket, looking at nothing.

“Pim!” shouted Bram.

The boy did not turn. He was holding a loaf in both hands, the Ferry Inn’s late order, and his face had the mild, sleepy look of someone who has walked into a room and cannot remember why.

The Greater Smudge rolled between them.

Tam had one Light left and no lamps between her and Pim but the last, lamp fifty-two, which stood dark on the far end of the bridge, right beside the boy. Kindle needed her to touch it. The Greater Smudge was in the way.

She thought, very fast, about what the ledger had said. Every lamp in this book has been lit by someone who wrote it down. She thought about three hundred and twelve years of names. She flipped the ledger open, not to the last page but to the very first, brown and soft and faded, and ran her finger down to the line she wanted.

14th of Frostmonth, lamp 52, lit by Agnes Wray.

Then she took her pencil and, underneath three centuries of other people’s names, wrote one more line. Lamp 52, lit again tonight, by Tamsin Reed, because Agnes Wray lit it first.

[System] Inscribe used on an unlit lamp. Unusual. Checking the Ledger... 312 years of entries found for Lamp 52. Lamp 52 remembers.

At the far end of the bridge, without anyone touching it, lamp fifty-two lit.

It did not light the way the others had. It lit all at once, as bright as a held breath let out, and the light it gave off was a deep and particular gold that Tam would have known anywhere: the colour of every lamp she had ever watched Mister Cobb light from her bedroom window, every night of her life. The Murk around it did not draw back. It simply stopped being there. The Greater Smudge went pale, then paler, and then it was a wisp of steam rising from the cobbles, and then it was nothing at all.

[System] Greater Smudge (Level 4) dispersed. Party gained 120 XP.

Pim blinked. He looked at the loaf in his hands, then at his brother running towards him across the bridge, then at the lamp.

“Bram,” he said, puzzled. “Why is it so late? I was only going to the Ferry Inn.”

Bram picked him up, loaf and all, and did not say anything for quite a long time.
