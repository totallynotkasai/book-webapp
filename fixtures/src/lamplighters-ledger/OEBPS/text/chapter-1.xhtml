Chapter 1 Dusk Shift

In Hollowmere, the System comes to you at dusk on your fourteenth birthday, and Tam Reed had always assumed she would be somewhere sensible when it happened. At home, perhaps, with her mother pretending not to watch. At the bakery on Wickery Lane, where the Ollerys handed out a free bun to anyone being Named that night. Anywhere but halfway up a ladder, with a hooked pole in one hand and a box of matches in her teeth.

But old Mister Cobb’s knees had finally given up, and somebody had to light the lamps.

The lamp at the top of the ladder was number forty-one, the one outside the cobbler’s, and its glass was furred with soot. Tam wiped it with her sleeve, turned the little brass key that let the oil rise into the wick, struck a match and held it in. For a breath nothing happened. Then the wick caught, and a small, gold, stubborn flame stood up inside the glass as if it had been waiting all day to be asked.

The bells of St Ember began to ring for sunset. And the air in front of Tam’s face turned the colour of deep water.

She nearly dropped the matches. The letters hung there, perfectly steady, while the ladder wobbled under her. Down in the lane a dog barked, a cart rattled past, and nobody else seemed to notice that a glowing blue square had just opened in the middle of the evening.

Tam read it twice, in case it changed. It did not. Her cousin Fen had been Named a Bargewright last spring, and the whole street had come out to cheer. The Pellow twins were both Clerks, which was dull, but Clerks got jobs at the Lumen Guild and wore grey coats with silver buttons. Nobody, as far as Tam knew, had been Named a Lamplighter in twenty years. There were only two lamplighters left in the whole of Hollowmere: Mister Cobb, who was seventy-three, and now her.

She touched the word Lamplighter with one finger. It felt like touching the surface of cold water. A second, smaller window slid out from underneath the first.

“A lamp I can touch,” Tam said to nobody. “I can do that with a match.”

She climbed down, moved the ladder six yards along the lane, and climbed up again. Lamp forty-two, outside the chandler’s. This time she left the matches in her pocket. She put her palm flat against the cold glass, felt rather foolish, and thought, very clearly, Kindle.

Something warm ran down her arm, the way warmth runs into your fingers when you hold a mug of cocoa. The wick inside the glass blushed orange, then gold, and lit.

[System] Kindle used. Light 2 / 3. You gained 5 XP.

Tam laughed out loud. A woman passing below with a basket of turnips looked up sharply, saw a girl grinning at a lamp, and decided it was none of her business.

The Wickery Round had fifty-two lamps. Mister Cobb had walked it every night for fifty years, and he had taught Tam the order the way some people teach poems: up Wickery Lane on the left, down on the right, over the Little Bridge, along the canal path as far as the Saltgate steps, back by the old wall. She had three Light and an ordinary box of matches, so she used Kindle where the wind was bad and matches everywhere else. By the time she reached the canal path, Kindle had gone up to Level 2, and the window had started to feel less like a stranger and more like a friend who talked too much.

The canal was the colour of pewter. On the far side, the windows of the Lumen Guild were lit with the Guild’s own sigil-lights, white and very bright and perfectly even, like a row of teeth. They never flickered. They never needed anyone to climb a ladder. Half of Hollowmere already had them. Mister Cobb said they were “very clever and very cold,” and then refused to explain what he meant.

Tam lit thirty-two, thirty-three, thirty-four. A grey cat with one white ear came out from under a coal barge and followed her along the path at a polite distance, the way cats do when they have decided something about you but are not ready to discuss it.

“I’ve got nothing for you,” Tam told it. “Unless you eat matches.”

The cat sat down beside lamp thirty-five and looked at it pointedly. Tam lit it. The cat seemed satisfied.

It was at lamp forty-eight, by the Saltgate steps, that the evening went wrong.

The lamp was already dark, which was not unusual; boys threw stones. But the glass was not broken. The wick was not burnt down. When Tam put her palm to it and thought Kindle, the warmth ran down her arm as before, reached her fingers, and simply stopped, as if it had come to a door that would not open.

[System] Kindle failed. Target is inside the Murk.

“Inside the what?”

The cat had gone very still. Its tail was twice its normal size.

Tam looked down the steps towards Saltgate Bridge, and for the first time she saw it properly. What she had taken for river mist was lying across the bottom of the steps like a grey blanket, thick and low and not moving with the wind. The lamps on the bridge beyond it were dark, every one of them. She could not hear the water under the bridge, although she knew it was there. She could not hear anything from that direction at all.

“Light that is remembered,” Tam read. “What does that mean?”

The window did not answer. Windows, she was learning, only answered the questions they wanted to.

A shape moved in the grey at the bottom of the steps. Just a smudge of darker grey, about the size of a loaf of bread, rolling slowly uphill against all sense. It reached the first step, and the stone under it lost its colour, the way a drawing loses its colour when you rub it with your thumb.

Tam did the only sensible thing, which was to take a match from the box, strike it, and hold it out at arm’s length.

The smudge stopped. It seemed to consider the match. Then it rolled backwards, down the step, into the blanket of grey, and was gone.

[System] You repelled a Smudge (Level 1). You gained 15 XP.

Her hand was shaking so badly that the match went out. She lit another one, and then she ran, with the cat running beside her, back along the canal path past thirty-five and thirty-four and all the lamps she had lit, which burned steadily and warmly and, she was almost sure, a little brighter than when she had left them.

She did not stop until she reached the narrow green door at the end of Candle Row, where Mister Cobb lived with his bad knees and his worse kettle. She knocked four times, which was the lamplighters’ knock. Inside, a chair scraped.

“It’s late for visitors,” said Mister Cobb through the door.

“It’s me. It’s Tam. I’ve been Named. And Saltgate Bridge has gone dark, and there’s something in the fog, and the System calls it the Murk, and the lamp at the steps won’t light.”

There was a long silence. Then the bolts slid back, one after another, more bolts than Tam had ever noticed, and the door opened. Mister Cobb stood there in his dressing gown with a candle in his hand. He looked at her face, and then at the cat, and then, for a long moment, at the empty air beside her shoulder, as if he could see the window still hanging there.

“Lamplighter,” he said softly. “Well, I never. You had better come in. I’ve been keeping something for you.”
