Chapter 6 Balancing the Ledger

“It’s going out,” said Bram. His voice had come back to him, but it was very small. “Tam, the ember’s going out.”

Tam knew. She could feel it through the brass of the lantern, the warmth draining out of it like water out of a cracked cup. Nine percent. Eight. Behind them the Warden of the Murk stood aside as it had promised, but the grey at the edges of the room was creeping in again, patient as ever, and the light of the ember was no longer enough to hold it back.

She thought about Kindle, and dismissed it. Kindle was something you did. The First Lamp wanted a flame that had been carried. She thought about matches, and dismissed those too. She thought, for one awful second, about the long walk back up the spiral stair with a cold lantern in her hand, and about Bram’s mother at the oven door raking out the ashes of a fire that had burned for eighty-one years.

Then she thought about bread.

“Bram,” she said. “What does Proof do?”

He stared at her. “It makes things rise.”

“Then make it rise.”

For a moment he did not understand. Then he did, and his whole floury face lit up, and he put down his end of the lantern and cupped both hands around the little brass door as if he were warming them at the last fire in the world.

“Proof,” said Bram Ollery, baker, very gently.

The coal in the lantern swelled. It went from dull red to cherry, from cherry to orange, and then, with a small soft sound like dough being turned out of a tin, a flame stood up from it: a real flame, as tall as Tam’s thumb, with a blue heart and a gold edge. It leaned towards the First Lamp the way a plant leans towards a window.

Tam opened the lantern door. She lifted out the flame on the end of a twist of wick, and she carried it the last three steps across the stone floor, and she touched it to the First Lamp’s dry black wick.

Nothing happened.

I burn for those written.

“Of course,” Tam whispered. “It isn’t lit until it’s written.”

She opened the ledger on her arm, to the hidden page at the back, and below Agnes Wray’s faded lines she wrote with her pencil stub, as neatly as she could with hands that would not stop shaking:

Lamp 0, the First Lamp, lit again tonight by Tamsin Reed, lamplighter, and Bram Ollery, baker, and Wick, cat, with the flame of the Ollery oven, which was lit from Lamp 1, which was lit from this lamp. Carried, not made.

[System] Inscribe used on Lamp 0. Checking the Ledger... 312 years and 113,958 nights of entries found. Every one of them was lit from this lamp. Lamp 0 remembers.

The First Lamp lit.

It did not light with a flash, or a roar, or anything dramatic. It lit the way a house lights up when someone comes home: all at once, warmly, as though the light had only been waiting for permission. The colour of it was the deep, particular gold of every lamp on the Wickery Round, the gold of lamp fifty-two on the bridge, the gold of every evening of Tam’s life, and it filled the round stone room to the walls.

The grey at the edges was gone. The Warden of the Murk was gone. Tam had not seen it go; there was only a faint smell in the air, like a page in an old book, and then not even that.

Above their heads, faintly, the bells of St Ember began to ring.

[System] World event: The First Lamp of Hollowmere has been relit. The Murk is retreating from the city.

When they climbed back up the spiral stair and out of the iron door, the old town was full of light. Every lamp along the old wall was burning, lamps that had stood cold for twenty years, lamps whose glass was cracked and whose posts leaned like tired old men. None of them had been touched. They had lit themselves, one after another, all the way out from St Ember tower, the way ripples go out from a stone dropped in a pond.

And the ledger in Tam’s arms was warm and heavy and, if she held it close to her ear, very faintly rustling, as page after page filled up with lines in a pale gold ink she had never seen before. Lamp 1, Old Town Round, lit from Lamp 0. Lamp 2, Old Town Round, lit from Lamp 0. Hundreds of them.

They walked home through streets that remembered their own names.

At the guildhall, Guildmaster Vell and forty Guild workers in grey coats were standing on stepladders in a long line down Wickery Lane, lighting oil lamps with ordinary matches and writing them, laboriously, in ordinary notebooks. When they saw Tam, a cheer went up and travelled all the way down the line. Mister Cobb was there too, on a chair someone had carried out for him, with his feet wrapped in a blanket and a mug of tea in his hand, and when Tam came up to him he did not say anything at all. He just held out his hand, and she gave him the ledger, and he looked at the hidden page for a long time.

“Agnes Wray,” he said at last. “And now Tamsin Reed. Well, I never.”

Tam said yes. Of course she said yes.

Guildmaster Vell found her a little later, sitting on the steps of the guildhall with Bram and two iced buns and the cat. The Guildmaster sat down beside them on the cold stone, which Tam would not have believed a week earlier, and for a while she simply watched the lamps.

“I have been thinking,” she said at last. “About the sigil-lights. They are good lights. They don’t slip on ice. But they need somebody to light them. Somebody who will remember.” She looked sideways at Tam. “The Lumen Guild would like to hire some lamplighters. Rather a lot of lamplighters, in fact. And someone to teach them, and someone to keep the book.”

“Mister Cobb should teach them,” said Tam. “He knows the Round like a poem.”

“Mister Cobb has already agreed, on condition that he is allowed to sit down.” The Guildmaster stood, and brushed off her grey coat, and almost smiled. “Goodnight, Keeper.”

When she had gone, Tam opened the ledger one more time, just to look at the hidden page and the new gold lines filling up the book. She meant only to look. But the ledger had other ideas. It fell open on its own, to a page right at the very end that had not been there before, and on it, in Agnes Wray’s faded handwriting, was a single line.

The flame came from the end of the Long Road. Someone there is still waiting for it to come back.
