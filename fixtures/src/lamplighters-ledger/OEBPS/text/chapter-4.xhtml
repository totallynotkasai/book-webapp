Chapter 4 Sigil-Lights

The notice went up on the guildhall door the next morning, printed on thick cream paper with the Lumen Guild’s silver seal at the bottom. By ten o’clock there was a small crowd in front of it. By eleven, someone had brought it to Tam.

PUBLIC NOTICE

By order of the Lumen Guild of Hollowmere:

The Wickery Round will be converted to sigil-lights, beginning tonight.

Oil lamps on the Round will be retired. The post of Lamplighter is no longer required.

Signed: Isolde Vell, Guildmaster.

“No longer required,” said Tam. She read it again. It said the same thing. Her window, which had been quiet all morning, put up a small grey line of its own at the bottom, as if to make sure she had understood.

[System] Your class has been marked Obsolete by a local authority. This has no effect on your skills.

“It’s got no effect on your skills,” said Bram, reading over her shoulder. He had come straight from the bakery with a bag of iced buns as a thank-you from his mother, and he was already on his third. “So that’s fine.”

“It’s not fine. They’re taking the lamps away.”

“They can’t take away the lamp on the bridge. You saw what it did.”

“Nobody saw what it did except us,” said Tam. “And Pim, and he thinks he had a dream.”

That was not quite true, as it turned out. At noon a Guild carriage stopped outside Mister Cobb’s green door, and a tall woman in a grey coat with silver buttons got out of it, alone. She knocked three times, sensibly, and when Mister Cobb let her in she did not sit down, because there was nowhere to sit that did not already have something on it.

“I am Isolde Vell,” she said. “I was watching Saltgate Bridge last night. I would like to know what you did to it.”

She was not what Tam had expected. Tam had expected someone sharp and smiling, like a villain in a puppet show. The Guildmaster looked tired. There were ink stains on her fingers, and her eyes kept going to the ledger on the table, the way your eyes keep going to a dog that might or might not bite.

Tam told her. She left out nothing, not even the part about Agnes Wray and the first page. When she had finished, Guildmaster Vell was silent for a while.

“Do you know why we build sigil-lights?” she said at last. “Because last winter a lamplighter on the Eastgate Round slipped on ice and broke his arm, and for six weeks nobody lit those lamps, and two people walked into the river in the dark. A sigil-light doesn’t slip on ice. It doesn’t get old. It doesn’t forget. I am trying to make a city where nobody has to climb a ladder in the snow so that other people can see where they’re going.”

“That’s a good reason,” Tam said, because it was.

“It is the only reason I have ever had.” The Guildmaster looked at the ledger again. “Show me.”

So that night, the Lumen Guild put up the first ten sigil-lights on Wickery Lane, on new silver posts beside the old iron ones, and Tam walked the Round as usual, and the Guildmaster walked it with her.

The sigil-lights woke on their own at sunset. They were beautiful, Tam had to admit: pale and perfectly round and very bright, so that the cobbles beneath them looked as clean as a scrubbed table. The Guild workers who had installed them stood about in their grey coats, looking pleased. Beside each one, Tam lit the old oil lamp anyway, with Kindle or a match, and wrote it in the ledger.

“The sigil-light is more than twice as bright,” said Guildmaster Vell, reading the table that Tam’s window obligingly showed them both.

“Yes,” said Tam.

“Then I don’t understand what you think will happen.”

“Neither do I,” Tam admitted. “Mister Cobb says the Murk eats light it doesn’t know.”

The Murk came up out of the canal a little after ten, much faster than the night before. It did not creep along the cobbles this time; it rose like a tide coming in over flat sand, grey and silent, and before Tam had finished writing lamp thirty-eight it was lapping around the posts at the bottom of Wickery Lane.

The first sigil-light went out at a quarter past ten.

It did not flicker. It did not dim. It was simply there and then not there, as if someone had closed a book on it. The second followed, then the third. The Guild workers shouted and ran about with their clipboards, and one of them tried to write something on a sigil-light post with a stick of silver chalk, which made no difference at all. Ten pale lights went out one after the other, all the way up Wickery Lane, like a row of eyes closing.

Beside every one of them, Tam’s small, stubborn, gold oil lamps went on burning. The grey drew back from each in a neat chalk circle, and in the circles the cobbles kept their colour, and the people who had come out of their houses to see what was going on stood inside the circles and remembered their own names.

[System] Inscribed lamps held: 38 / 38. Uninscribed lights lost: 10 / 10.

Guildmaster Vell stood very still under lamp twelve. In the gold light her face looked younger, and much more worried.

“Can you write one of mine in your book?” she asked quietly. “A sigil-light. If you wrote it down, would it stay?”

Tam tried. She stood beside the cold silver post, opened the ledger, and wrote: Lamp 12-S, lit by, and then stopped, because she did not know how to finish the sentence. Nobody had lit it. It had lit itself.

[System] Inscribe failed. This light has no lighter.

“A light that nobody lit,” said the Guildmaster slowly, “is a light that nobody remembers.” She gave a short, unhappy laugh. “Ten years of work, and the answer was in an old man’s book on a shelf above his fire.”

Tam was not listening. The ledger had grown warm under her hand, much warmer than usual, and the window was doing something she had not seen before. It was flickering at the edges, gold and blue, gold and blue, like a candle in a draught.

[System] Level up! You are now Level 5. A hidden page in the Ledger has been revealed.

She turned to the back of the book. Where there had been the inside of the cover, brown and blank, there was now a page. The writing on it was so old and so faded that she could only read it by holding it very close to lamp twelve.

Lamp 0. The First Lamp. Beneath the tower of St Ember. Lit by Agnes Wray with a flame carried, not made, from the end of the Long Road. All the lamps of this city are lit from it. Keep it, and they will keep you.

Below it was a small, careful drawing: the clock tower of St Ember, a stair going down beneath it, and at the bottom of the stair a lamp, drawn without a flame.

QUEST OFFERED

Name: Relight the First Lamp

Difficulty: Hard

Recommended party: 3

Requirement: A flame carried, not made.

Reward: ???

Accept? [Yes] [No]

Tam looked up from the page. Around the bottom of Wickery Lane the Murk was still rising, patient and grey, filling the spaces between the chalk circles of light. In the direction of the old town, where the clock tower of St Ember should have been standing black against the stars, there was nothing at all.

“It went out,” she said. “The First Lamp. That’s where the Murk is coming from. Somebody let it go out.”
